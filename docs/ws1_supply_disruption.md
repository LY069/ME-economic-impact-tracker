## Narrative

### (a) Pre-war vs. current flow loss, by product

**Crude oil** is the best-documented product and shows a genuine but partial recovery from the
March 2026 nadir, with estimates of "current" flow diverging sharply depending on what is being
measured:
- **EIA** (hard, primary source): Hormuz crude+petroleum-liquids throughput fell from a 4Q25
  baseline of **21.6 mb/d** to just **4.9 mb/d** averaged over 2Q26 — a **−77%** collapse.
- **IEA's September 2026 Oil Market Report** puts total **Gulf** crude exports (i.e., including
  barrels that never transit the strait, having moved via the Saudi and UAE bypass pipelines) at
  **~13 mb/d in August**, "nearly half" the pre-war level — i.e., crude losses have "narrowed to
  just below 45%."
- **Kpler's "reconstructed" Gulf exports** for early September are higher, at **15–16 mb/d**,
  still 7–8 mb/d below pre-war.
- **Goldman Sachs** (2026-08-28) put Hormuz flows even higher, at "two-thirds of pre-war levels."
- At the other extreme, **vessel-transit counts** (as opposed to volume estimates) show the
  strait itself still nearly empty: just **7 vessels** transited on 2026-09-09 (10-day average
  ~13–14/day) against a pre-war baseline the same trackers put at ~125/day — a **~89–94%**
  decline in vessel count that is far steeper than the ~33–50% decline in estimated volume.
  This gap is explained by larger average cargo sizes, ship-to-ship transfers, and — critically —
  the growing share of Gulf crude that reaches export terminals via **pipeline** (Saudi
  East–West/Yanbu, UAE Habshan–Fujairah) rather than by tanker through the strait at all. The
  shared brief's own baseline (53.2 tanker-transits/day pre-war, falling to ~2/day at the trough
  and "~3% of pre-war levels" as of 2026-09-11) is consistent with this tanker-specific,
  transit-count framing rather than the broader "Gulf exports" volume framing used by IEA/Kpler/
  Goldman. **Readers should not directly compare a "% of pre-war" figure from one source against
  another without checking which of these three things it is measuring.**
- The US administration's own claim (Energy Secretary Wright, 2026-08-31: >17 million barrels
  moved through the strait "the highest since the war began") sits well above independent tanker
  trackers, which estimate the true run-rate closer to 5–10 mb/d for the same period — a gap
  Al Jazeera's reporting attributes to covert transits and STS transfers that official volume
  claims may be double-counting or extrapolating from a single strong day.

**LNG** has fared far worse than crude and shows comparatively little recovery. LNG cannot be
shuttled via pipeline or easily transferred ship-to-ship in the way crude can, so Qatar's
exports — which depend almost entirely on the strait — collapsed by **~96%** in cargo-count
terms in the first six months of the war (18 cargoes in H1 2026 vs. 509 in H1 2025) and remained
down **57.6%** to China and **64.3%** globally even through July 2026, the most recent hard data
found. A single QatarEnergy-linked cargo (Al Marrouna) transiting Hormuz on 2026-09-10 was
reported as the *first* such shipment since late July — underscoring how thin the flow remains
even six months in. Force majeure notices to Pakistan and Bangladesh buyers have been extended
into October/November. China's annual LNG imports are forecast to fall 6–11% in 2026 versus
2025, a smaller percentage decline than the bilateral Qatar figures because China has
diversified toward other suppliers (Russia, Australia, US) and drawn on record stockpiles.

**LPG/condensate**: Gulf LPG exports (~1.2–1.5 mb/d pre-war, ~29% of all Hormuz-transiting
volume by one UNCTAD-cited estimate) are not tracked with a clean current-vs-baseline pair in
available sources; the clearest signal is on the **price** side (see below), where Saudi CP
propane has risen 15–43% depending on the comparison month, evidence of a persistently tighter
market even as some volume has resumed. Condensate-specific volume data (distinct from crude)
was not found in this session's searches and is marked NA.

**Refined products**: IEA's September OMR figure of **390 kb/d** of Gulf diesel/gasoil net
exports in August, described as "just over a quarter" of pre-war levels, is the one hard,
dated figure available; no broader refined-products aggregate (gasoline, jet, fuel oil) with a
clean pre-war baseline was found.

**Petrochemicals/fertilizer** (methanol, MEG, urea, ammonia, sulphur): all five show the same
qualitative pattern — a severe supply-driven price spike in March–April 2026 (methanol exports
essentially halted for ~18–20 Mt/yr of Middle East capacity per one source; Qatar suspended
urea/ammonia/sulphur production after facility damage; Iran halted ammonia production; >600kt of
sulphur was stranded on vessels; global sulphur exports fell 45% below end-February levels) that
had substantially unwound by June–August 2026 as some Gulf capacity restarted and stranded
cargoes crossed the strait (800kt+ of sulphur reported crossing in June). Urea, having peaked at
~$850/mt in April (+80% vs. an implied ~$472/mt pre-war level), had eased to $453/mt by June.
Ammonia peaked near $520/mt in July before easing to $470/mt by late August. This is a materially
different recovery shape than crude or LNG: the physical-volume shock for petrochemicals looks to
have been sharper but shorter, likely because much of Gulf petchem output can be — and was —
substituted with stranded-cargo release and demand destruction rather than requiring a durable
alternate export route the way LNG does.

### (b) Bypass capacity and the September 11 East–West pipeline shutdown

Two pipeline systems do the heavy lifting of the current partial-bypass regime:

- **Saudi Arabia's East–West Crude Oil Pipeline ("Petroline")**: 7 mb/d nameplate capacity,
  running to the Red Sea port of **Yanbu** (combined North+South nominal loading capacity ~4.5
  mb/d). By late March 2026 Saudi Arabia had ramped the line to ~5 mb/d of actual crude flow
  (plus 700–900 kb/d of refined products from Yanbu), i.e., roughly 70% utilisation of nameplate.
  The line was hit and briefly disrupted once before, in April 2026 (restored to full capacity
  within days), and was hit again by **drone strikes launched from Iraqi territory on
  2026-09-10/11**. The **Saudi Ministry of Energy confirmed on 2026-09-11 that the pipeline had
  been shut down "as a precautionary measure,"** which — per the shared brief — forced all
  crude that would otherwise use it back through the strait and was linked to headline figures of
  ~39% of global oil trade and 31% of global shipments being disrupted, with Hormuz transit
  falling to "~3% of pre-war levels." **Critically, this analyst could not independently confirm
  the pipeline's operating status as of 2026-09-14** (today): one search result attributed to
  Argus/the energy ministry describes "full pumping capacity restored... after a strike on one
  of its pumping stations cut ~700,000 b/d," which is plausible for the September incident but
  could not be reliably distinguished in the sources retrieved from reporting on the *earlier*,
  separate April 2026 restoration. Muddying this further, Vortexa and Kpler both report **Yanbu
  loadings continuing in the 2.9–3.7 mb/d range in early-to-mid September** — activity that is
  hard to reconcile with a pipeline reported fully shut on September 11 (it may reflect cargoes
  loaded from tank farm inventory before the shutdown, a partial/short-lived outage, or a
  restoration that occurred within the September 11–14 window that this session's searches did
  not pin down with a clean date). **This is flagged as the single largest live uncertainty in
  this report** and should be re-verified before being treated as settled either way.

- **UAE's Habshan–Fujairah pipeline (ADCOP)**: 1.5–1.8 mb/d capacity, running to the Fujairah
  terminal on the Gulf of Oman (outside the strait). It has been running at or near full
  nameplate utilisation since the war began — April 2026 loadings of 1.82 mb/d represented 80%
  of *all* UAE crude loadings, up from just 30% pre-war. A second, parallel pipeline that would
  roughly double this capacity to ~3.6 mb/d is under accelerated construction, ~50% complete as
  of 2026-05-20, targeting 2027 completion — i.e., **no near-term additional UAE bypass capacity
  is coming before this report's as-of date.**

- **Iraq–Turkey Kirkuk–Ceyhan pipeline**: theoretical capacity 1.5 mb/d, but actual throughput
  has been running at only **170–200 kb/d** even after Iraq and Turkey signed a fresh one-year
  transit deal on 2026-08-01 guaranteeing a 750 kb/d minimum — i.e., the pipeline is running at
  roughly **11–13% of nameplate** and well below even its own contracted minimum, making it a
  minor and underused bypass route relative to its potential, for reasons (Kurdistan-Baghdad
  revenue-sharing disputes, infrastructure condition) not resolved by this war.

- **Saudi Red Sea/Yanbu** is covered above as the East–West Pipeline's terminus; it is the single
  largest bypass outlet in the system by a wide margin.

Taken together, bypass pipeline capacity (Saudi + UAE, nameplate) is roughly **8.5–8.8 mb/d**
(7 mb/d East–West + 1.5–1.8 mb/d Habshan–Fujairah), against a pre-war Hormuz-transiting volume
of ~20–21.6 mb/d — meaning even at full utilisation of every bypass route, roughly
**11–13 mb/d** of pre-war flow has no pipeline alternative and must either transit the strait
directly, be replaced by demand destruction/stockdraw (as China and Japan have both done), or go
unmet. The September 11 shutdown of the single largest bypass route (Saudi East–West) — even if
temporary — removes up to 7 mb/d of that already-limited redundancy at a moment when Houthi
forces were simultaneously completing their takeover of the Bab el-Mandeb strait on the Red Sea
side (2026-09-11-12), which is the outlet the East–West Pipeline feeds into. That is a materially
adverse combination for the reliability of the one bypass route capable of carrying the largest
single volume.

### (c) Price response, by product

Prices confirm a market that has been volatile and elevated throughout, with a renewed leg up
in the days immediately preceding this report:

- **Crude**: Brent has round-tripped several times — from ~$71/bbl pre-war to a ~$118/bbl peak
  (2026-03-02), back down toward $84/bbl by 2026-08-10, and then **up again to $100–105/bbl by
  2026-09-09/11** as the East–West pipeline attack, Bab el-Mandeb seizure, and the 2026-09-13
  Qeshm Island tanker attack compounded. The Brent–Dubai (EFS) spread — a direct read on how much
  extra the market is paying for non-Gulf-origin barrels versus Gulf sour crude stuck behind the
  bottleneck — widened from under $2/bbl pre-war to as much as $16.25/bbl at points in the
  Jun–Jul 2026 window, though no clean current (September) point value was found.
- **LNG**: JKM has more than doubled from a ~$9–14/MMBtu pre-war range to **$24.8–24.9/MMBtu**
  on 2026-09-11 (+119% y/y), the steepest relative move of any product tracked here, consistent
  with LNG's near-total loss of the ability to bypass the strait.
- **LPG**: Saudi CP propane has been on a saw-tooth — $542/mt (Feb) → $750/mt (Apr, +38%) →
  $760/mt (Jun peak) → $580/mt (Jul) → **$625/mt (Sep CP) / $775/mt (Sep Far East swap)** — with
  OPIS explicitly attributing the September CP increase to "renewed US-Iran strikes."
- **Naphtha**: Japan CFR naphtha stood at ~$789–855/mt around 2026-09-11/14 (two slightly
  different index readings); no clean pre-war comparator was found, so the percentage move is
  not calculable from available sources.
- **Refined-product cracks are the outlier**: the Singapore ULSD gasoil crack to Dubai hit a
  **record low** of $2.70/bl on 2026-09-03, and the Gasoil EFS spread was at –$142.01/mt on
  2026-09-08 — i.e., even as crude itself remains supply-constrained and expensive, downstream
  refined-product margins in Asia are historically weak on oversupply and soft demand. This is an
  important nuance: **the crude/LNG/petchem supply shock and the refined-product demand/margin
  picture are moving in different directions**, and headline "Hormuz crisis" framing should not
  be read as implying uniform tightness across the whole barrel.
- **Fertilizer/petrochemicals**: urea, ammonia, methanol and sulphur all show the same
  April-peak/mid-year-moderation shape described in (a) above; MEG is the exception, with
  MEGlobal's September 2026 Asian Contract Price ($780/mt) sitting well above the Q2 2026 China
  spot level ($553/mt), suggesting MEG has NOT moderated to the same degree as urea/ammonia.
- **Freight and insurance costs remain at or near record highs and, unlike refined-product
  cracks, show no sign of easing**: the VLCC TD3C index reached **$759,969/day in early
  September 2026 — 26% above** its already-extreme mid-March peak of ~$420,000/day, and vessels
  actually transiting the strait itself are reportedly commanding charter premiums above
  $500,000/day. War-risk insurance premiums have been flat at **7.5–10% of hull value** since
  July, roughly 60–80x the pre-crisis 0.125% baseline, with no reported easing through
  2026-09-03 — the most recent point found. Marine insurers Gard and Skuld withdrew ancillary
  Red Sea/Gulf of Aden war-risk cover altogether on 2026-08-16, tightening capacity further just
  as the Bab el-Mandeb corridor became contested.

### (d) Tightening or easing, as of 2026-09-14?

**On balance, the evidence points to renewed tightening over the two weeks immediately preceding
this report's as-of date, layered on top of a partial, uneven recovery that had been underway
since roughly June–July 2026.** The case for each:

*Evidence of the prior recovery (June–August 2026), now at risk:*
- Hormuz-adjacent volume estimates (IEA, Kpler, Goldman) all show meaningful — if
  inconsistent — improvement from the March trough, converging in an admittedly wide 50–67%-of-
  pre-war band for August/early-September Gulf export volumes.
- The Saudi and UAE bypass pipelines had reached high utilisation (East–West at ~5–7 mb/d, ADCOP
  at ~1.82 mb/d, ~80% of UAE loadings).
- Fertilizer/petchem prices (urea, ammonia) had fallen substantially from their April peaks.
- Brent itself had round-tripped down to ~$84/bbl by 2026-08-10, and EIA even framed early July
  as an "opening" of the strait, briefly.

*Evidence of fresh, active tightening in the days right before 2026-09-14:*
- The **2026-09-10/11 drone attack and shutdown of the Saudi East–West Pipeline** — the single
  largest bypass route — is the most consequential event in this window and its current
  operating status is unresolved (see Uncertainties).
- The **Houthis' completion of their takeover of the Bab el-Mandeb Yemeni coastline and capture
  of Mayyun/Perim Island (2026-09-10 to 09-12)** directly threatens the Red Sea corridor that the
  East–West Pipeline (via Yanbu) depends on — a second chokepoint now under contest at the exact
  moment the first bypass route was hit.
- Hormuz vessel-transit counts fell to a fresh low of **7/day on 2026-09-09**, half the already-
  depressed 10-day average.
- Brent rose from ~$93/bbl (Sept 2) to **$104.61/bbl (Sept 11)** — a ~12% jump in nine days.
- The **2026-09-13 attack on an Iranian cargo vessel near Qeshm Island and the resulting
  postponement of the Oman-brokered Iran–Gulf shipping-protection talks** removes, for now, the
  one active diplomatic track that might have stabilized the security situation; JKM LNG and
  VLCC freight are both at or near record highs with no sign of relief; war-risk premiums remain
  at their July peak with insurers actively withdrawing capacity (Gard/Skuld, Aug 16).

**Read**: the underlying trend from March through August 2026 was a genuine, if partial and
contested, easing of the physical disruption as bypass infrastructure ramped up and traders
adapted. That trend reversed course in the first two weeks of September 2026: the East–West
pipeline shutdown, the Bab el-Mandeb seizure, and the Qeshm Island attack constitute three
compounding shocks to the *routes around* Hormuz — not just to the strait itself — arriving
within a five-day span (Sept 9–13). Prices (Brent, JKM, VLCC freight) and vessel-transit counts
had already begun moving in the tightening direction before this report's as-of date. Whether
this is a durable reversal or a short-lived spike (as the July 8 → July 27 episode showed can
happen) cannot be determined from the evidence gathered in this session; the pipeline's
unconfirmed post-shutdown status is the single most important fact that would resolve this
question and should be the first thing re-checked.

## Uncertainties and gaps

- **East–West Pipeline post-shutdown status (highest priority gap).** Shut down 2026-09-11 per
  the Saudi Energy Ministry; this session could not independently confirm whether it has since
  been restored, partially restored, or remains down as of 2026-09-14. Available search results
  plausibly describing a restoration could not be reliably disambiguated from reporting on the
  separate, earlier April 2026 incident/restoration. Yanbu loadings data from Vortexa (3.7 mb/d)
  and Kpler (2.9 mb/d) for early/mid-September appear inconsistent with a fully shut pipeline,
  but the two trackers also disagree with each other by ~28%, and neither is dated precisely
  enough to establish a clean timeline. **This should be re-verified before being used in any
  onward analysis or headline claim.**
- **"% of pre-war Hormuz flow" is not a single number.** This session found at least four
  materially different current estimates (IEA ~13 mb/d/~50%; Kpler ~15–16 mb/d/~65-70%;
  Goldman ~67%; US government ~40-50% i.e. 8-10mb/d vs ~20mb/d baseline; independent tanker
  trackers ~25-35% i.e. 5-7mb/d), which are not all measuring the same thing (Hormuz-transiting
  volume only vs. total Gulf export volume including pipeline-diverted barrels). Vessel-transit
  counts (7-14/day vs. ~125/day baseline, i.e. ~6-11%) are a fifth, even lower figure, because
  larger average cargo sizes and STS transfers mean fewer vessels now carry a similar volume.
  **We trust the EIA (4.9 mb/d, 2Q26 average) and IEA (~13 mb/d Gulf exports, Aug'26) figures
  most, as they are the two primary/quasi-primary institutional sources with transparent
  methodology notes; Kpler/Goldman/tracker estimates are presented as a supporting range, not a
  replacement.**
- **No September 2026 price print was found** (despite targeted searches) for: urea, methanol
  (USD-denominated), Kuwait sulphur KSP, the current Brent-Dubai EFS point value, VLCC TD3C
  pre-war baseline, and current LNG carrier charter rates. These are marked `NA` in the CSVs per
  the brief's instruction never to invent a number; the most recent confirmed prior data point is
  cited instead with its own date.
- **Japan's pre-war Hormuz-transit crude share is reported inconsistently across sources found in
  this session** — 93% in one framing, 73.7% in a METI-attributed figure from the same reporting
  window (January 2026) alongside a 95.1% Middle-East-overall figure. Both are recorded in the
  flows CSV; the discrepancy was not resolved and may reflect different definitions (physical
  strait transit vs. country-of-origin).
- **China's Hormuz-dependency baseline also varies** — ~35% of total crude supply vs. ~40-51% of
  seaborne imports specifically, per different Vortexa/other citations found in the same search
  session, including one source revising its own historical figure (51%→44%) without full
  explanation. The shared brief's ~40% anchor sits within this range.
- **Condensate** volumes specific to Hormuz (as distinct from crude generally) were not
  separately found and are marked NA throughout.
- **LPG in physical mb/d/mt-yr terms** for pre-war vs. current Hormuz-specific flow was not
  found with a clean paired baseline/current figure; only 2025 baseline export estimates (1.2-1.5
  mb/d, two different framings from the same source) were located. The price-side evidence
  (Saudi CP propane) is more solid and used as the primary indicator of tightness for this
  product.
- **Source-quality flag**: several aggregator sites (e.g., stellarix.com, discoveryalert.com.au,
  theworldreviews.com) surfaced in searches with plausible-sounding but undated or unsourced
  figures (e.g., the "sulphur $525→$910/mt, +70%" claim). These are marked `est` with an explicit
  "shaky source" note in the CSV rather than being dropped, since they were directionally
  consistent with the hard Kuwait KPC data, but should not be treated as authoritative without
  independent confirmation from Argus, ICIS, or a primary producer announcement.
- **Session search budget was exhausted** before several planned follow-up queries (current EFS
  point value, exact East–West pipeline restart date/confirmation, current sulphur/methanol/urea
  prints, LNG charter current rate) could be run. These are the highest-value next searches for
  a follow-on session.
