### 3. Supplier concentration (HHI) — illustrative `calc`

Formula: **HHI = Σ(supplier share, in percentage points)²**, summed over named suppliers plus one residual "other" bucket. Because the residual bucket is itself several smaller suppliers lumped together, **treating it as a single item inflates the calculated HHI above the true value** — these are directional/upper-bound estimates only, not precision figures. (Standard reference points: HHI <1500 = low concentration, 1500-2500 = moderate, >2500 = high, on the 0-10,000 scale.)

- **Japan crude, H1 2025** (UAE 44, Saudi 40, Kuwait 7, Qatar 4, other 5): HHI = 44²+40²+7²+4²+5² = **3,626** → high concentration (two suppliers, UAE+Saudi, are 84% of imports).
- **Japan LNG, H1 2026** (Australia 42, Malaysia 16, US 10, Russia 9, ME 11, other 12): HHI = 42²+16²+10²+9²+11²+12² = **2,466** → moderate-to-high, dominated by single-country (Australia) exposure.
- **China crude, 2025 FY** (Russia 17.8, Saudi 14, Iraq 12, Malaysia+Brazil combined 18.2, other 38): HHI = 17.8²+14²+12²+18.2²+38² = **2,432 (upper bound)** → true HHI is materially lower given China's much larger supplier count (Angola, Oman, UAE, Kuwait, Brazil, Malaysia, US, West Africa all present); qualitatively China's crude sourcing is the most diversified of the two countries.
- **China LNG, 2025 FY** (Qatar ~27.8%, Australia ~27.6%, Russia ~10.2%, Malaysia ~9.7%, other ~24.7%, converting tonnage to an assumed ~73.5 mnt total import base at 1 mtpa≈1.36 bcm — `calc`, assumption-dependent): HHI = 28²+28²+10²+10²+25² ≈ **2,343 (upper bound)**.

**Takeaway**: Japan's crude HHI (~3,600) is materially higher than China's (~2,400, itself an overstatement) — Japan is more concentrated in fewer Gulf suppliers, with UAE+Saudi alone at 84%.

## Narrative

### Japan
Japan's exposure to the Iran war is close to a worst case among major economies for the oil side of the shock and structurally severe for LNG. Japan has essentially zero domestic crude production, imports ~95-100% of its oil, and — going into the war — sourced roughly 95% of that from the Middle East, concentrated in just two suppliers (UAE and Saudi Arabia, ~84% combined in H1 2025). Refineries are configured for medium-sour Gulf crude; industry and refiner reporting indicates a historical blending ceiling of only 10-20% non-Middle-East crude without yield/margin penalties, pushed to an estimated 30-50% under duress (at the cost of less diesel/jet output and higher feedstock cost). Japan responded with its largest-ever SPR release (~80 mn bbl from 2026-03-16, further releases in April) and an emergency pivot to US light-sweet WTI — the Middle East share of crude imports fell from ~95% pre-war to **59.3% by July 2026**, with the US share rising to 36.3%. This is a genuine and rapid diversification, but April 2026 saw Middle East crude imports at their lowest level since 1979 records began (-67.2% y/y), evidencing real disruption to refinery throughput in the interim (refiners reportedly ran at ~two-thirds capacity in April 2026 awaiting non-Gulf crude). By August 2026 total import *volume* had recovered to roughly 2025's average, but the September 2026 outlook is for imports to fall back to ~80% of normal as the lengthened Cape-of-Good-Hope routing (10-14 extra days per leg, cutting effective tanker fleet availability by roughly a third) continues to bind, following the 2026-09-11 East-West Pipeline shutdown that pushed all remaining Saudi crude back through Hormuz.

On gas, Japan's LNG book is less Gulf-concentrated at the point of origin (only ~11% directly from Oman/Qatar/UAE) but still Hormuz-exposed at the margin because 83% of all Gulf LNG and 93-96% of Qatari/UAE LNG specifically transits the strait, and because the March 2026 Iranian strike on Qatar's Ras Laffan complex permanently destroyed ~17% of Qatar's global LNG export capacity (Trains 4 and 6, 12.8 mtpa) with an estimated 3-5 year repair timeline (large-frame gas-turbine supply is a bottleneck: only three global manufacturers, 2-4 year lead times). Japan's LNG imports from Qatar fell 56% half-on-half into H1 2026, largely absorbed by leaning further into Australia (42% share, near the historical record of 43%). Because gas/LNG supplies ~31-34% of Japan's power generation (vs. coal ~28-32%, nuclear ~10%, solar ~11%), this is Japan's most structurally important lever: nuclear restarts are the primary domestic offset available, and the fleet has grown to **15 operating reactors (of 32 operable)** as of the 2026-02-09 restart of Kashiwazaki-Kariwa Unit 6, with 3 more reactors holding initial regulatory approval, 6 under review, and 8 with no restart application filed — meaning further nuclear-driven LNG-demand relief is available but slow-moving (Japan's FY2040 target of ~20% nuclear share needs up to 30 reactors online, roughly double today's count).

Downstream, Japan's naphtha-fed petrochemical sector is the most visibly stressed segment: naphtha crackers were already running at a record-low 75.1% utilization pre-war (March 2025) on structural overcapacity, and the war compounded this — the government confirmed only "four months" of naphtha coverage in April 2026 and three major producers (Mitsubishi Chemical, Asahi Kasei, Mitsui Chemicals) have since agreed to merge/consolidate steam-cracker capacity (951,000 t/yr combined ethylene). LPG has been the one clear Japanese success story: historical Middle East dependence has been diversified down to ~10% of imports, with the US/Canada/Australia now supplying 91% — a diversification largely completed *before* this war, insulating Japan's ~24-25 million LPG-using households (about a third of all households) from the worst of the shock.

### China
China's exposure is lower in aggregate but sharper in specific chemical-feedstock niches. China's crude sourcing is materially more diversified than Japan's: no single supplier exceeded ~18% of 2025 imports, Russia (pipeline-heavy, largely Hormuz-immune) is the #1 supplier at 17.8%, and the Gulf-5 (Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Iraq) combined account for a more moderate 36.1% of the total. China also holds real physical hedges Japan lacks entirely: ~4.3-4.6 mn b/d of domestic crude production (vs. Japan's near-zero), an ESPO pipeline from Russia with ~35 million tonnes/year (~700 kb/d) of Hormuz-immune capacity, Power of Siberia 1 gas pipeline deliveries of 38.8 bcm in 2025 (with an agreed step-up to 44 bcm/yr), coal reserves that are the world's third-largest (13.5% of global proven reserves) feeding an 84%+ overall energy self-sufficiency rate, and gas/LNG supplying only **~2.9%** of China's power generation (vs. Japan's ~31-34%) — meaning the electricity sector is barely exposed to the LNG side of the shock at all; coal (the dominant fossil input to China's grid) is a domestic, non-Hormuz commodity. Consistent with this resilience, China used SPR/commercial-stock drawdowns to let crude imports fall to an extraordinary degree (below 8.0 mn b/d in May-June 2026, the first sub-8 mn print since 2016) without rationing power, and is now gradually rebuilding (Aug 2026: 8.93 mn b/d, +6.2% m/m, though still -23.4% y/y and -14.6% y/y cumulatively over Jan-Aug).

Where China is acutely exposed is in narrow, high-value petrochemical feedstocks that have no domestic or diversified substitute at scale: **methanol** (Iran alone supplied 60.42% of China's 2025 methanol imports — the single most concentrated dependency identified anywhere in this study — and the March 2026 collapse removed an estimated 700-900 kt/month from global supply) and **sulphur** (45-56% of China's 9.6 mnt 2025 imports from the Middle East, feeding phosphate-fertilizer and metals processing; China's policy response has been to cut sulfuric-acid *exports* — from 4.6 mnt in 2025 to a projected ~1.2 mnt in 2026 — rather than to substitute imports, implying limited near-term substitution capacity). China's LNG book also saw a sharp compositional shift: Qatar's share of China's LNG imports collapsed from a 2025 co-leading position (~28% by tonnage, `calc`) to just 13.3% in Jan-Jul 2026 (-57.6% y/y), with Australia stepping in as the new top supplier (35.6% share, a record). China is a large net urea *exporter* (capacity >77 mnt/yr) — the opposite exposure profile to Japan — so global urea/ammonia disruption is a market-share opportunity for China more than a domestic-supply risk, though this study could not source a China-specific ammonia import dependency figure (marked NA).

### Asymmetry verdict
**Japan is more exposed on energy (crude oil and power-sector gas)**; **China is more exposed on select chemical feedstocks (methanol, sulphur) and comparably exposed on LPG**, but has far more structural insulation overall (pipelines, domestic crude and coal, a power grid barely reliant on gas, and the world's largest strategic + commercial stock buffer). Japan has no pipeline options at all (island nation), a refinery fleet physically configured around Gulf medium-sour crude that cannot easily re-slate, and a power sector roughly ten times more gas-dependent than China's — these are binding physical constraints, not just statistical exposure. China's methanol and sulphur numbers are a reminder that "diversified in aggregate" can still mean "critically concentrated in specific inputs," and both countries share a common vulnerability in the shipping layer itself: the 2026-09-11 shutdown of the Saudi East-West Pipeline forced essentially all remaining Gulf crude back through Hormuz, and war-risk insurance premia — which the shared brief's baseline placed at ~1% of hull value by spring 2026 — had **re-escalated to 7.5-10% of hull value by late July/August 2026**, an order of magnitude above the pre-war 0.125% and roughly 3-10x the brief's spring reading. This is flagged as a material update to the established baseline that should be corrected upstream.

## Uncertainties and gaps

- **Japan crude/LNG import-dependency ratios**: no single authoritative hard figure was found for the precise current (2026) import-dependency ratio (imports ÷ consumption) for crude or LNG; used IEA/EIA-sourced 97% (2022) as a floor and treated LNG as ~100% (qualitative, near-zero domestic production) — both marked `est`, not `hard`.
- **Japan naphtha ME share**: two credible estimates conflict (74% of naphtha imports, FY2024, vs. ~60%, 2025) — range retained and flagged rather than resolved.
- **Japan urea/ammonia, sulphur**: could not source current import volume, value, or Middle-East-specific share for any of these three products for Japan; marked `NA`. This is a genuine gap — Japan's ammonia exposure (used for DEF/AdBlue and as a co-firing fuel) is plausibly significant given known Saudi-Japan ammonia supply-chain MOUs, but no 2025/26 quantified share was found via search.
- **China LPG current (2026) Middle East share**: only the 2025 full-year figure (45%) was found; no update for 2026 located.
- **China MEG**: exact Saudi Arabia % share and China-specific volume of Middle East MEG imports not found; only global Middle East export volume (>6.5 mnt, 2025) with a qualitative "much of it to China."
- **China sulphur ME share**: two sources disagree (ICIS ~50% vs. S&P Global 56.2%) — both retained in the CSV, true value likely in the 50-56% band.
- **China LPG supplier figures**: one source (OPIS) cites H1-2025 GCC export volumes (UAE 6.8mnt, Qatar 4.9mnt, Saudi 3.1mnt) that are larger than full-year-2024 China-specific import volumes (UAE 3.7mnt, Qatar 2.5mnt) from a different source (Hellenic Shipping News) — these appear to describe different universes (GCC total exports to all destinations vs. China-specific imports) and should not be conflated; flagged in the CSV rather than reconciled, since reconciliation would require a primary customs source not surfaced by search.
- **HHI calculations**: all four HHI figures in this brief are `calc` and explicitly approximate/upper-bound, because "other" residual supplier shares had to be lumped into a single bucket rather than broken out — see methodology note in Key Figures §3.
- **Jet fuel/gasoil**: neither country's direct import dependency for finished jet/gasoil was separately sourced; both countries refine the bulk of these products domestically from imported (mostly Middle Eastern, for Japan) crude, so exposure is treated as indirect via the crude line rather than quantified separately. Marked `NA` for direct product-level import data.
- **China crude "% via Hormuz" vs. "% from Gulf suppliers"**: these are not identical (some Gulf-sourced barrels move by pipeline/alternate routes, and China receives meaningful non-Gulf-but-still-Hormuz-transiting volumes are not indicated); the 36.1% Gulf-5 supplier-share figure is used as the best available proxy, distinct from the shared brief's ~40% Hormuz-transit anchor — both are shown, not reconciled, since the underlying methodologies differ.
- **Currency/value data**: total import *value* (not just volume) was inconsistently available across products; where only value was found (e.g., China crude supplier $ figures) it is retained, but a consistent volume-and-value matrix across all nine products was not achievable from search alone within the allotted budget.
- **Source quality**: several 2026-vintage figures rely on secondary aggregators (IndexBox, DiscoveryAlert, etc.) rather than primary METI/GACC/JODI releases, because WebFetch was unavailable and primary-source statistical portals were not reliably indexed by search; these are marked `est` and flagged inline per source. A follow-up pass with direct access to METI/ANRE (Japan) and GACC/NBS (China) portals would upgrade several `est` rows to `hard`.
